# Research and Analysis Writing Style

## Purpose

Write or edit deep-research and analytical articles so the reader can quickly see the author's judgment, understand the evidence behind it, and know what could change it. Use this profile after the research itself has produced enough material; it governs the article's reasoning and expression rather than the evidence-gathering workflow.

Typical uses include company comparisons, investment analysis, industry research, product or policy analysis, and evidence-based decision notes.

## Core Standard

The article should be **decisive, concise, and evidence-aware**:

- State the current conclusion in the opening, including a ranking or choice when the question calls for one.
- Use evidence to support the conclusion instead of surrounding it with generalized explanation.
- Preserve genuine uncertainty, but express it as a precise boundary or update condition.
- Give each paragraph one job. Keep it only if it changes the reader's understanding, supports a judgment, defines scope, or identifies information that would change the conclusion.

## Three Governing Principles

### 1. Prefer affirmative judgments over defensive phrasing

Frequent constructions such as `不能……`、`不要……`、`无法证明……` make analytical prose sound as though it is continually arguing with an imaginary reader. Rewrite them as direct statements about what the evidence does show, which time horizon it covers, or which criterion governs the judgment.

Examples:

- Instead of `公司质量和买入性价比不能用同一套排名代替`，write `公司质量排序为 A ＞ B ＞ C；当前买入性价比排序为 C ＞ B ＞ A。`
- Instead of `财报后的上涨不能作为长期排名的证明`，write `财报后的上涨反映短期预期差；长期排名取决于盈利质量、现金流和资本配置。`
- Instead of `某公司不能因为单日下跌就排在最后`，write `该公司的综合排名由经营趋势、估值和现金流共同决定，当前排在第二。`

Negative phrasing remains appropriate when it is the clearest factual statement. The goal is to reduce repetitive rebuttal and prohibition, not to ban particular words.

### 2. Give the conclusion plainly

Analytical writing exists to make a judgment, not merely to display the research process.

- Answer the main question before explaining the path to the answer.
- When several criteria produce different rankings, name each criterion and give each ranking directly.
- If the evidence supports a best current answer, state it even when uncertainty remains.
- Attach the most important qualification to the conclusion in one sentence. Move secondary qualifications to the relevant section or remove them.
- Use confidence language precisely: `当前首选`、`暂列第二`、`证据较强`、`仍需验证` are more useful than vague balance such as `各有优劣` or `需要综合来看`.

A reader should be able to quote the author's position after reading the opening and still find the same position at the end.

### 3. Remove broad or non-functional prose

Delete sentences that sound reasonable but add no decision value. Common examples include generic research-background narration, repeated reminders to stay objective, exhaustive evidence disclaimers, and abstract transitions such as `值得注意的是`、`从长期来看`、`需要综合考虑` when the following sentence already carries the meaning.

Use this deletion test for every paragraph:

1. Does it support or change a conclusion?
2. Does it provide evidence the reader needs?
3. Does it define a comparison criterion or factual boundary?
4. Does it identify a concrete condition that would change the conclusion?

If all four answers are no, remove it.

## Recommended Flow

Use the shortest structure that fits the material. A comparison article will often need only:

1. **Direct conclusion** — one short paragraph with the main ranking or choice.
2. **Comparison criteria** — a compact table when different criteria produce different answers.
3. **Core evidence** — only the figures and facts that drive the judgment.
4. **Subject judgments** — one section per company, option, or case: conclusion first, evidence second, one decisive watch point last.
5. **Final decision** — a short synthesis that resolves the original question.

Add methodology, evidence hierarchy, market reaction, unresolved questions, or update triggers only when they materially help the reader assess or use the conclusion.

## Evidence and Uncertainty

- Distinguish facts, interpretations, and opinions through clear wording rather than repeated disclaimers.
- Keep source references attached to material claims when the format supports citations.
- Use a caveat only when it changes the ranking, decision, confidence, or monitoring plan.
- Convert a long risk list into the two or three variables most likely to alter the conclusion.
- Treat short-term market reactions as evidence about expectations; use operating results and capital allocation for long-term judgments.
- Preserve conflicting evidence when it is genuinely important, then explain which evidence receives more weight and why.

## Avoid Repetition

The executive summary, ranking table, company sections, and final conclusion should reinforce one another without restating identical sentences.

- The opening gives the answer.
- The table maps criteria to rankings.
- The body explains why.
- The ending resolves the decision.

If two sections perform the same job, merge or remove one of them.

## Editing Existing Analysis

- Preserve the author's actual conclusion and degree of conviction unless the evidence in the supplied material contradicts it.
- Identify the main question and write the current answer in one or two sentences before editing the rest.
- Remove duplicated qualifications, research chronology, and wide background sections first.
- Replace long `不能……` or `不要……` sentences with affirmative judgments and explicit criteria.
- Keep facts, source links, dates, and calculation notes intact unless the user asks for factual revision.
- After editing, compare length and scan for repeated conclusions, empty transitions, defensive phrasing, and unsupported certainty.

## Self-check

Before returning the article, verify:

- Is the main conclusion visible in the opening?
- Is every requested ranking or choice stated explicitly?
- Can the reader distinguish company quality, current valuation, and other comparison criteria?
- Does each paragraph earn its place through judgment, evidence, scope, or a decision-changing condition?
- Have repetitive negative constructions and imaginary rebuttals been reduced?
- Are the key caveats precise and located beside the claims they qualify?
- Do the beginning and ending express the same position without unnecessary repetition?
- Were any facts, certainty, or personal opinions invented?

If the article still feels cautious but unclear, rewrite the conclusion first and then remove the paragraphs that no longer contribute to it.
